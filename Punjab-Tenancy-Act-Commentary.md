# Punjab Tenancy Act, 1887

## Section-by-Section Litigation Commentary

### Punjab & Haryana Practitioner Edition

---

# Introduction

The Punjab Tenancy Act, 1887 remains the foundation of agricultural tenancy law in Punjab and Haryana.

Most litigation involving:

* Gair Marusi tenants
* Occupancy rights
* Ejectment
* Rent disputes
* Agricultural possession
* Proprietary rights

ultimately traces back to provisions of this Act. The Act contains a dedicated chapter on occupancy rights (Sections 5–11) and provisions dealing with rent, relinquishment, and ejectment of tenants.

---

# Chapter I – Preliminary

## Section 4 – Definitions

This is one of the most important provisions in practice.

### Important Definitions

#### Tenant

A person who holds land under another person and is liable to pay rent.

#### Landlord

Person entitled to receive rent.

#### Rent

Whatever is payable by tenant to landlord for use and occupation of land.

---

# Litigation Note

Always determine first whether the person is:

* Owner
* Occupancy Tenant
* Non-Occupancy Tenant
* Gair Marusi
* Licensee
* Mortgagee in Possession

Many cases fail because the status of the occupant is incorrectly assumed.

---

# Chapter II – Right of Occupancy

## Sections 5 to 11

This chapter forms the heart of tenancy litigation.

The Act recognizes classes of tenants who acquire protected occupancy rights and also preserves existing occupancy rights recognized under earlier law.

---

# Section 5 – Tenants Having Right of Occupancy

## Importance

Most occupancy-right litigation begins here.

The section recognizes categories of tenants who may acquire occupancy rights based upon historical possession, hereditary cultivation, and statutory conditions.

---

## Practical Questions

### Question 1

Was claimant a tenant?

### Question 2

How long has tenancy existed?

### Question 3

Was possession hereditary?

### Question 4

Do historical records support occupancy status?

---

## Evidence Required

### Strong Evidence

* Settlement records
* Misal Haqiat
* Wajib-ul-Arz
* Early Jamabandis

### Supporting Evidence

* Mutation records
* Rent entries
* Girdawari records

---

## Litigation Strategy

### For Tenant

Prove:

* Continuous possession
* Historical tenancy
* Recognition in old records
* Satisfaction of statutory requirements

### For Landowner

Challenge:

* Origin of possession
* Continuity
* Status as tenant
* Historical record support

---

# Section 5(2) – Presumption Based Upon Long Occupation

This subsection is frequently invoked in Haryana and Punjab litigation.

Courts examine whether statutory conditions of long and continuous occupation have been fulfilled before any presumption can arise. Numerous reported cases turn on proof of continuous occupation and the precise requirements of Section 5(2).

---

# Section 6 – Existing Occupancy Tenants

## Principle

Persons already recognized as occupancy tenants before earlier legislation may continue to enjoy such rights.

---

## Practical Significance

Always inspect:

* Earlier settlement records
* Historical jamabandis
* Customary records

---

# Section 7 – Exchange Land

Occupancy rights may continue in land received through exchange under prescribed circumstances.

---

# Section 8 – Occupancy Rights on Other Grounds

## Importance

One of the most important sections for creative litigation.

The section preserves claims based on:

* Custom
* Usage
* Other legal grounds

and makes clear that occupancy rights are not limited only to situations expressly listed in Sections 5–7.

---

## Practical Use

Frequently pleaded where:

* Customary rights exist.
* Revenue records are incomplete.
* Historical occupation predates available records.

---

# Section 9 – Mere Passage of Time

## Fundamental Rule

Occupancy rights are not acquired merely because time has passed.

Long possession alone is insufficient without satisfaction of legal requirements.

---

## Common Mistake

Many litigants argue:

"Possession for 50 years equals ownership."

The Act rejects this approach.

---

# Section 10 – Joint Ownership

## Principle

A joint owner cannot automatically acquire occupancy rights merely because he uses a portion of jointly owned land.

---

## Interaction with Co-Sharer Law

This provision works alongside the principles later developed in co-sharer jurisprudence:

* Possession of one co-sharer is possession of all.
* Exclusive use does not necessarily create exclusive title.

---

# Section 11 – Continuance of Existing Occupancy Rights

## Purpose

Protects occupancy rights already existing under prior law.

Existing lawful rights continue unless legally extinguished.

---

# Chapter III – Rent

## Sections 12 onwards

These provisions regulate:

* Rent
* Produce rent
* Cash rent
* Recovery of rent
* Adjustment of rent

The Act recognizes multiple methods of determining rent including produce share, fixed rates and lump-sum arrangements.

---

# Practical Revenue Entries

## Batai

Share of produce.

---

## Chakota

Fixed rent arrangement.

---

## Cash Rent

Monetary payment.

---

## Bila Lagan

Without rent.

---

# Litigation Importance

The rent column frequently determines:

* Whether tenancy existed.
* Nature of tenancy.
* Occupancy claims.

---

# Relinquishment of Tenancy

## Practical Principle

A tenant wishing to surrender tenancy must comply with statutory requirements.

Failure may continue liability for rent.

---

# Ejectment of Tenants

## Importance

One of the most litigated portions of the Act.

The Act does not permit arbitrary ejectment. Occupancy tenants enjoy stronger protection and ejectment generally requires statutory grounds and due process.

---

## Occupancy Tenant

Generally enjoys strong protection.

---

## Non-Occupancy Tenant

Protection exists but is comparatively weaker.

---

## Gair Marusi

The outcome depends on:

* Status proved
* Revenue entries
* Nature of tenancy
* Applicable statutory protection

---

# Revenue Court Jurisdiction

Many tenancy disputes fall within the jurisdiction of revenue courts under the statutory scheme.

Always determine:

* Whether issue concerns tenancy.
* Whether declaration of ownership is sought.
* Whether civil court jurisdiction is barred or concurrent.

---

# Punjab Occupancy Tenants (Vesting of Proprietary Rights) Act, 1952

## Connection with Punjab Tenancy Act

Many occupancy tenants later became proprietors under vesting legislation. The Vesting Act defines landlord and occupancy tenant by reference to the tenancy relationship and forms the bridge between occupancy status and proprietary ownership.

# Key Legal Propositions

1. Occupancy rights are statutory rights.
2. Long possession alone does not create occupancy rights.
3. Mere lapse of time does not create occupancy rights.
4. Historical records outweigh recent entries.
5. Rent entries are often decisive.
6. Gair Marusi does not automatically become owner.
7. Existing occupancy rights continue unless lawfully extinguished.
8. Ejectment requires statutory authority and due process.

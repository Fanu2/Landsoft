# Part I – Core Principles of Punjab & Haryana Revenue Law

## Introduction

This handbook is intended for lawyers, revenue practitioners, landowners, researchers, and software developers working with Punjab and Haryana land records.

The focus is on:

* Gair Marusi tenants
* Occupancy tenants
* Khudkasht and Khanakasht land
* Co-sharer rights
* Partition proceedings
* Revenue records
* Consolidation disputes
* Mutation and title issues

---

# Chapter 1 – Sources of Land Rights

Land rights in Punjab and Haryana are determined primarily through:

1. Statutory law
2. Revenue records
3. Judicial precedents
4. Consolidation records
5. Historical settlement records

The most important statutes include:

* Punjab Land Revenue Act, 1887
* Punjab Tenancy Act, 1887
* Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act, 1961
* East Punjab Holdings (Consolidation and Prevention of Fragmentation) Act, 1948
* Punjab Occupancy Tenants (Vesting of Proprietary Rights) Act, 1952
* Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953
* Haryana Ceiling on Land Holdings Act, 1972

---

# Chapter 2 – Revenue Records Hierarchy

Not all revenue records carry equal evidentiary value.

## Tier 1 – Highest Value

### Misal Haqiat

Contains original settlement information and historical rights.

### Wajib-ul-Arz

Village administration and customary rights.

### Settlement Records

Frequently decisive in tenancy litigation.

---

## Tier 2 – Strong Evidence

### Old Jamabandis

Especially pre-consolidation records.

### Mutation Registers

Useful for tracing title history.

---

## Tier 3 – Operational Records

### Current Jamabandi

Presumption of correctness but not conclusive.

### Khasra Girdawari

Shows cultivation and possession.

---

## Tier 4 – Oral Evidence

Generally weaker than documentary evidence.

---

# Chapter 3 – Meaning of Gair Marusi

The expression "Gair Marusi" generally indicates a non-occupancy tenant.

Important points:

* Gair Marusi does not automatically mean owner.
* Gair Marusi does not automatically mean occupancy tenant.
* Gair Marusi does not automatically mean adverse possessor.

Every case depends upon:

* Origin of possession
* Rent entries
* Duration of possession
* Historical records

---

# Chapter 4 – Occupancy Tenants

Occupancy tenants enjoy stronger protection than ordinary tenants.

Characteristics:

* Heritable rights
* Statutory protection
* Potential entitlement under vesting legislation

Evidence required:

* Long-standing tenancy
* Historical recognition
* Settlement records
* Revenue entries

---

# Chapter 5 – Khudkasht and Khanakasht

## Khudkasht

Land cultivated directly by the proprietor.

## Khanakasht

Historical expression generally indicating self-cultivation.

These entries often become decisive in tenancy disputes.

When historical records repeatedly show:

* Khudkasht
* Khanakasht
* Makbuja Malkan

claims of protected tenancy become difficult to establish.

---

# Chapter 6 – Co-Sharer Rights

The most important proposition:

> Every co-sharer owns every inch of joint land.

Consequences:

* Possession of one co-sharer is possession of all.
* Exclusive cultivation does not necessarily create exclusive ownership.
* Ouster is not easily presumed.

---

## Leading Authorities

### Bhartu v. Ram Sarup

Foundational Full Bench authority.

### Sant Ram Nagina Ram v. Daya Ram Nagina Ram

Classic authority on co-sharer possession.

### Jai Singh v. Gurmej Singh

Important Supreme Court authority.

---

# Chapter 7 – Ouster of Co-Sharer

Ouster requires proof of:

1. Hostile possession.
2. Open denial of rights.
3. Knowledge of excluded co-sharers.
4. Long continuous exclusion.

Mere possession is insufficient.

---

# Chapter 8 – Sale by Co-Sharer

A co-sharer frequently sells:

* Specific khasra number
* Specific killa number
* Specific field

Legal position:

The purchaser generally acquires the rights of the vendor co-sharer.

The sale remains subject to partition.

---

# Chapter 9 – Partition Proceedings

Partition is governed primarily by the Punjab Land Revenue Act.

Objectives:

* Separate shares.
* Allocate possession.
* Remove disputes regarding enjoyment.

---

## Revenue Officer May Decide

* Extent of shares
* Mode of partition
* Allocation of parcels

---

## Revenue Officer Usually Does Not Decide

* Complicated title disputes
* Fraud claims
* Ownership declarations

Such matters generally belong to civil courts.

---

# Chapter 10 – Mutation

Mutation serves fiscal purposes.

Important principle:

Mutation does not:

* Create title
* Destroy title
* Transfer title by itself

Title must arise independently.

---

# Chapter 11 – Khasra Girdawari

Girdawari records:

* Cultivation
* Crop details
* Possession

Girdawari does not by itself prove ownership.

---

# Chapter 12 – Adverse Possession

## Against Landlord

Claimant must prove:

* Hostility
* Knowledge
* Continuous possession
* Denial of title

---

## Among Co-Sharers

Particularly difficult.

Courts require clear proof of ouster.

---

# Chapter 13 – Practical Litigation Checklist

Always obtain:

* Latest Jamabandi
* Oldest available Jamabandi
* Mutation chain
* Khasra Girdawari
* Misal Haqiat
* Wajib-ul-Arz
* Consolidation records
* Aks Shajra
* Roznamcha entries

---

# Key Principles Summary

1. Revenue entries are evidence, not title.
2. Gair Marusi does not automatically become owner.
3. Occupancy rights require proof.
4. Co-sharer possession is presumed joint.
5. Ouster is difficult to prove.
6. Mutation does not create title.
7. Partition is the normal remedy for co-sharer disputes.
8. Historical records often outweigh current records.
